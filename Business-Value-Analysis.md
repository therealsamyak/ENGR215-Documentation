## Core Value Proposition

Ships are blind sideways. Hull-mounted sonar only looks forward and down. Anything submerged a kilometer or two to either side goes completely undetected until it is too late. The *Ever Given* grounding in the Suez Canal cost the global economy roughly $9.6 billion per day for six days. Ship owners paid hundreds of millions in damages, legal fees, and lost contracts.

Aegis Swarm fixes this with a 2 km real-time underwater perimeter made up of 10 to 20 cheap, relay-linked drones. The value breaks down into three areas:

- Risk reduction: ships get lateral underwater visibility they have never had before. Hazards are detected before they become collisions.
- Cost savings: survey vessels run $20,000 to $50,000 per day. A single traditional AUV costs $250,000+, and operators are scared to actually deploy them in hazardous waters. An entire Aegis Swarm costs less than 10% of one military-grade AUV. Individual drones are disposable. Lose one, the other 19 keep working.
- Time savings: ships currently wait days for military sweepers or cleared windows before transiting chokepoints. Aegis Swarm lets them scout on their own schedule.

There is also an indirect benefit that does not get talked about enough. Even when insurance covers collision damage, the vessel sits in dry dock for months. That is lost revenue and missed contracts that insurance does not replace. Prevention beats a payout.


## Revenue Model

Aegis Swarm uses a pure leasing model. No upfront hardware purchase. Customers pay a monthly fee that covers the full system: drone hardware (10 to 20 units per vessel), the bridge-mounted command unit, detection software, maintenance, and replacement drones when units are lost.

The payers are fleet operators at companies like Maersk, MSC, and Euronav, as well as cruise lines. Pricing is roughly $8,000 to $15,000 per month per vessel depending on swarm size and route risk. For reference, one day of a survey vessel costs $20,000 to $50,000. A full month of Aegis Swarm costs less than a single day of the traditional option.

Leases run on annual contracts with monthly billing. Customers keep paying because hazard detection is not a one-time thing. Ships deal with shifting debris, uncharted obstacles, and congested ports on every transit. The detection software also improves with use; more deployment data makes the AI more accurate over time.

Leasing makes sense for us because traditional AUVs require a $250,000+ capital purchase, and that is a hard sell for a new product in a conservative industry. Leasing turns a capital decision into an operational one and shifts hardware risk from the customer to us. The drones are supposed to be disposable. The pricing model should reflect that.


## Customer Payment Logic

The *Ever Given* blocked the Suez Canal for six days. Trade losses hit $9.6 billion per day. The ship owner's direct costs ran into the hundreds of millions. Even with full insurance coverage, the vessel was out of service for months. Insurance pays for repairs. It does not pay for the contracts you lost while the ship sat in dry dock.

This is a real problem that ship operators already pay for through elevated war-risk insurance premiums and convoy compliance in hazardous areas. But current options for actually doing something about it are bad:

- Do nothing and accept the risk. This is what most operators do today.
- Hire survey vessels at $20,000 to $50,000 per day. Too expensive for routine use.
- Buy a traditional AUV for $250,000+. Operators hesitate to risk expensive equipment in the exact hazards they bought it to scout.
- Wait for military escorts or cleared windows. Costs days of delays, burned fuel, and wasted crew time.

At $8,000 to $15,000 per month, a full year of Aegis Swarm costs about the same as one week of a survey vessel. The lease covers everything: hardware, software, maintenance, replacements. For a fleet of 50+ vessels, the monthly total is a rounding error compared to the cost of a single grounding. This is not theoretical ROI. It is the difference between a $500 million disaster and a $15,000 per month system that prevents it.


## Scale Potential

The problem is global. Every major shipping chokepoint (Straits of Malacca, Suez, Panama, Hormuz, Bosporus) is a recurring hazard zone. Port approaches in developing nations are often poorly charted. Any vessel transiting any of these routes is a potential customer.

Our targets are fleet operators, not individual ships. Maersk runs 700+ vessels. MSC runs 800+. A single enterprise contract covers hundreds of ships. Once proven on a pilot fleet, rollout is natural because the bridge software and crew training are standardized. Revenue scales linearly with vessel count.

The leasing model is recurring by design. Every active vessel generates monthly revenue for as long as it operates in hazardous waters. Removing the system means going back to blind transit, so churn should be low.

The same hardware and software stack applies to adjacent markets: cruise ships (thousands of passengers, even stronger safety incentive), luxury yachts (low tolerance for any damage), and offshore oil and gas platforms (continuous perimeter monitoring need).

The detection AI also gets better with scale. Every deployed swarm feeds data back into the central model. More deployments mean better detection and fewer false positives. Software improves at near-zero marginal cost.


## Business Model Risks

Weak willingness to pay is the biggest risk. Commercial operators treat underwater hazard management as someone else's job. Port authorities, government agencies, and insurers handle it. Even if they agree the risk is real, the default reaction is "we already have systems for this." Getting them to pay for onboard hardware means proving direct cost savings, not just safety improvements. Reduced delays, lower insurance premiums, and avoided downtime need to be quantifiable and obvious.

Regulatory barriers are a second problem. IMO SOLAS requires electronic charts (ECDIS) but says nothing about active scouting. There is no mandate pushing adoption. Our workaround is to partner with insurers who can offer premium reductions for Aegis-equipped vessels, which creates a financial incentive even without a regulatory one.

Third, we carry the hardware cost. In a leasing model, we build and deploy swarms before monthly revenue covers the expense. If adoption is slow, the cash flow gap between production costs and incoming lease payments could be painful. This is worst in the early stages before manufacturing costs come down with volume.

Fourth, the concept is easy to copy. Cheap disposable underwater drones are not deeply patentable. If we prove demand, companies like Lockheed Martin, Boeing, and Thales have the resources to build competing products. Our advantage has to come from speed, proprietary sensor integration, real deployment data, and relationships with shippers and insurers.

Fifth, we depend on third-party suppliers for sensors, communication components, and possibly satellite connectivity. Supply chain disruptions or supplier price hikes eat into margins. We design for hardware flexibility and avoid single-source suppliers where possible, but this risk does not fully go away.


## Business Model Summary

Aegis Swarm sits in a gap that currently has no product: between doing nothing about underwater hazards and spending $250,000+ on an AUV you are afraid to use. The leasing model removes the capital barrier, turning a six-figure safety investment into an $8,000 to $15,000 monthly operational cost that is cheaper than one day of the existing alternative. Revenue scales through fleet-wide enterprise contracts, recurs monthly, and the detection software gets better with every deployment. The main risk is whether commercial operators will pay for a problem they have always treated as someone else's. The path forward is straightforward: prove cost avoidance on a pilot fleet, partner with insurers to create financial incentives, and let the numbers drive adoption.
